Ten ways to sort Shellby's settings

Settings today is five tabs on one long page. You have to know which tab a setting lives in, and the tabs are named after where things came from (Around you, Connections) rather than what you're trying to do. Search helps only if you already know the setting's wording.

Each mockup below is a different answer to "where would I look first?" They're drawn at the panel's real size, 460 by 700, in his own colours and type. Most are clickable.

Mockup 1

Ask first, file later

The opening screen is a short list of questions in your words, and each one tells you what it's set to now. You can see your whole setup without opening anything, and you only go a level deeper when you want to change something.

"You changed lately" puts the thing you fiddled with yesterday one tap away.

Fixes
Tab names that only make sense once you've learned them. Scrolling past 60 controls to find one.
Costs
One extra tap for everything. Each summary line has to be kept up to date by code.

Click "How he looks and acts".

Mockup 2

A rail down the side

Nine areas sit in a thin rail that's always there, so you can jump between them without scrolling back to the top. Inside an area, each group's heading sticks as you scroll, so you always know where you are.

The filter chips are the main draw. "Changed" shows only what you've moved off the default, marked with a coral dot. "Off" shows what you haven't tried.

Fixes
Losing your place in a long scroll. Not knowing what you've changed.
Costs
Takes 64px of a 460px panel. Nine one-word labels have to carry a lot.

Open the interactive version, with every setting in it.

Mockup 3

Just type it

Settings opens with a search box, and the box understands what you mean as well as what you type. "Quieter", "stop climbing" or "phone" each find every setting that does that, wherever it lives, and you can change it right there in the result.

Each result shows the path to it, so you learn where things live as you go. When the box is empty it shows what you changed lately and what other people change most.

Fixes
Today's search only matches exact wording, and you still have to change the setting in its tab.
Costs
Someone has to write and keep up the list of other words for each setting. Doesn't help anyone who just wants to look around.

Try "phone", "sound", "tests", "git" or clear the box.

Mockup 4

Essentials first

Most people only ever touch about a dozen settings. Put those on one screen you never have to scroll, and tuck the other 195 behind a single, honest "195 more".

Shellby already counts which screens you open. It could count which settings you change and move your own favourites into Essentials over time.

Fixes
A new user facing 207 controls on day one. Matches what the bar already does by adding screens as you go.
Costs
Power users will go straight to Everything, so it still has to be well sorted.

Mockup 5

A mixing desk for his personality

About 30 of today's switches answer one question: how lively should he be? So answer it once with a preset, then nudge five sliders. Each slider stands for a handful of today's switches, and "fine-tune" opens them.

This grows out of Work mode, which is already a preset in all but name.

Fixes
The Shellby tab's 31 controls, most of which people only ever change together.
Costs
Only covers personality. Claude and code settings still need sorting some other way.

Pick a preset, then move a slider.

Mockup 6

Point at him

The crab is the menu. His shell is how he looks, his voice is talking and sound, his legs are moving and climbing, his claws are what Claude may do, his eyes are what he notices around you, and "beyond the desk" is your phone, Discord and other PCs.

It's the one idea here that only Shellby could have. It works best as the first level, with a plain list behind each part.

Fixes
Makes Settings feel like part of him, and is easy to remember once seen.
Costs
Some settings don't belong to a body part (updates, crash reports), so a plain "System" link is still needed. Needs search beside it.

Click the labels around him, or open the interactive version.

Mockup 7

Sorted by what leaves the PC

Half of today's fine print is explaining what a feature reads and where it goes. This mockup sorts by that instead: stays here, uses the internet, runs things. Each line says what goes where in a line of type rather than a paragraph.

It isn't meant to be all of Settings. It's a second view, "What Shellby can see and do", that sits next to the main one and covers every feature that touches your data.

Fixes
The long privacy paragraphs under every switch. Gives a privacy-minded user one place to check.
Costs
Some settings would show up in two places. Look and sound settings don't fit at all.

Mockup 8

Read it like a sentence

Your setup, written as a short paragraph about him, with each underlined word a choice you can change. Reading it tells you exactly how things stand, without decoding a column of switches.

It suits his voice: Shellby already talks about himself in plain sentences. A "List" switch keeps the ordinary layout for people who want it.

Fixes
Seeing your whole setup at once. Settings that only make sense together, like volume and background sound.
Costs
Gets long fast, so it only works for the 30 or so main choices. Harder to translate.

Change any underlined word.

Mockup 9

In the order things happen

The Claude tab is 64 controls with no clear order. But almost all of them come into play at one point in a task: before it starts, while it runs, when it ends, while you're away, or when the copy comes home. Laying them along that path makes "where is the tests setting?" easy: it's at the end of a turn.

The numbers are earned here, because these are real steps in order.

Fixes
The most crowded tab. Shows why a setting matters by showing when it kicks in.
Costs
Only fits Claude and code settings. His look, accounts and system need their own pages.

Mockup 10

Connections as a shelf

The Connections and Around you tabs, plus the Claude Code everywhere section, are really fifteen separate add-ons. Show them as a shelf of tiles: the ones you've connected at the top, the rest below. Tapping one opens just its settings in a sheet.

Each add-on's setup steps (the QR code, the GitHub device code, the four-step profile card) live inside its own sheet instead of filling the page for everyone.

Fixes
The biggest tab, 69 controls, most of which only matter once you've set that thing up.
Costs
Changing something deep inside an add-on takes a tap more. Works as one area of Settings, not all of it.

Click a tile.

What I'd build

Combine them rather than pick one. Use the nine areas from 1 as the base, open Settings on the search box from 3 with the "Changed" filter from 2, turn his personality into the presets from 5, and turn Apps and devices into the shelf from 10. Keep 6 (point at him) and 7 (what leaves the PC) as extra ways in. They're the memorable parts, and they work best on top of a plain structure.
